Where five years of spend went, and who carried it

An analysis of 68.6 million claims across 1.26 million members, 2020–2024.

Findings only. Recommendations follow in a later pass.

About Calder Health

Our mission: keep comprehensive cover affordable for the people we insure — and keep it affordable in the places they actually get care.

Calder is a non-profit health plan headquartered in Cleveland, serving 1.26 million members with concentrations in Chicago, Detroit and Northeast Ohio. We run two lines of business, and they are designed differently:

  • Commercial — employer group plans, built on a deductible and coinsurance with an annual out-of-pocket maximum.
  • Government — Medicaid and Medicare managed care, built on a flat copay per visit.

That difference turns out to matter more than almost anything else in this report. The same condition can cost a commercial member ten times what it costs a government member, and it is our own benefit design doing it.

The brief. Before we set benefits and negotiate networks for 2026, understand three things: where the money went, who bore it, and where it concentrates.

How to read these numbers

This analysis runs on simulated claims data, and some of it is not realistic. Read the shape, not the price tag.

Trust: rankings, shares, ratios, and how concentrated things are. Which conditions dominate, who pays what proportion, how few sites carry the spend — these hold up and are the basis of everything below.

Do not trust: absolute dollar amounts. A pregnancy bills 8.6× what it costs in reality. Do not quote a per-case figure from this report to anyone.

Cannot be answered here at all: anything about negotiated rates, denials, bad debt or collections. In this data every claim is paid in full, and no provider is charged a different price from any other. Those are properties of the simulation, not findings about us.

Executive summary

  • Twenty conditions carry 91% of the bill we can attribute to a diagnosis — and pregnancy alone is 39.8% of it. Our spend is not spread thin; it sits in a handful of places.
  • Members carry a fifth of the total, and it lands hardest on the cheapest care. They pay 41.6% of a wellness visit and 8.6% of an inpatient stay. The inexpensive, routine things are what people actually feel.
  • Which plan a member holds matters more than what is wrong with them. Prediabetes costs a commercial member 85.0% of the bill and a government member 8.5% — the same care, a ten-fold difference, and it is our design that creates it.
  • Spending concentrates in places, not in people. 86 of 3,918 sites carry half the money, but it takes 134,198 members to reach the same half. There is no small group of high-cost members to manage.
  • Our most expensive hospitals are not charging more. Reprice every procedure identically and the gap between sites barely moves — only 7.8% of a site’s cost per visit is its prices. They differ in what they treat, not what they charge.

What we could not answer

Stated plainly so that silence is not mistaken for a clean bill of health.

  • Whether members skip preventive care. We checked. Wellness visits reach 1,259,199 of our 1,259,375 members, and the small variation that exists tracks who is in each group rather than what they received. The data schedules these visits by protocol, so it cannot show anyone declining or missing care. The answerable neighbour of this question is Finding 2. (q3_preventive_access.sql)
  • Whether any hospital is efficient. Every difference between sites resolves to what they treat. Prices barely vary, and neither does the amount of work done for a given condition.
  • Anything about denials, collections or bad debt. Every claim in this data is paid in full.
  • Whether our members are sicker or healthier than the market. The population is generated, not observed, and its very sickest members are under-represented compared with real claims data.

The findings

Finding 1Twenty conditions carry nearly all of the bill, and one of them is pregnancy

What this shows. Of the $72.7B we can attach to a specific diagnosis, twenty conditions account for 90.8% — and 66.6% of everything we spend. Normal pregnancy alone is 39.8%, more than the next three conditions combined. Ranking by cost per member reorders the list completely: gingivitis spreads $10,661 across 800,465 members, while lung cancer concentrates $1,416,145 into 2,384.

Why it matters for Calder. It tells us where to look and, just as usefully, where not to. A programme aimed at the long tail of conditions would be working on a third of our spend at most. It also warns against a single approach: gingivitis is expensive because it reaches almost everyone, lung cancer because it is intense for very few. Those are different problems that happen to sit on the same list.

What we examined

  • Total billed per condition, ranked, against total billed per member — the two rankings disagree sharply and the disagreement is the point.
  • Whether pregnancy at 39.8% is credible. The number of visits per pregnancy is plausible (11.1, against a real-world 10–15); the price per visit is not.
  • Whether the top twenty are extreme on one driver or several. They are moderately extreme on all three at once — members reached, visits each, cost per visit — and the multiplication does the rest.
  • Whether hospitals differ in what they charge for the same condition. For eight of the ten largest, barely at all.

Finding 2Members carry a fifth of the bill, and it falls hardest on the cheapest care

What this shows. Members bear 20.4% of all spend — $20.3B, or $16,084 each across five years. The burden runs opposite to cost: they pay 41.6% of a wellness visit and 8.6% of an inpatient stay. Across types of care the same inversion holds — blood disorders sit at 38.9% of a $121M bill, cancer at 8.3% of a $7.1B one.

Why it matters for Calder. This is our mission measured directly, and it reads badly. The care a member pays most for is the routine, inexpensive, easily-deferred kind — a check-up, a filling, a screening. The care we protect them from is the catastrophic kind they cannot defer anyway. Dental is the clearest single case: 27.9% of an $11.83B bill, falling on 938,009 people.

What we examined

  • Member share by type of care, with total bill and number of people alongside — share alone says nothing about size.
  • Whether the pattern survives inside one line of business, or was an artefact of mixing them. It strengthens: the relationship is clearer within commercial and within government than across both together.
  • The two care types that appeared to break the pattern. Neither does — one is skewed by a handful of very large claims, the other is 90.2% government-funded.
  • Why the mechanism differs by line of business, which is Finding 3.

Finding 3Which plan a member holds matters more than what is wrong with them

What this shows. For every one of the ten conditions where members carry the most, commercial members pay a larger share than government members — never by less than 12.9 points. Prediabetes is the extreme: 85.0% for a commercial member against 8.5% for a government one, for identical care. Over five years a government member pays $2,118 out of pocket, a commercial member $19,178, and an uninsured one $61,742.

Why it matters for Calder. This gap is ours. It is not the market, not the providers, not how sick anyone is — it is the difference between a flat copay and a deductible with an annual maximum. Cheap routine care never accumulates enough in a year to reach a commercial member’s out-of-pocket maximum, so they pay coinsurance on all of it; a government member pays their copay and nothing more. Any figure we quote as “what members pay” describes no actual member.

What we examined

  • The ten highest-burden conditions, split three ways, using the typical year rather than five years pooled so the range is visible.
  • Whether the gap is stable or a single bad year. It holds in all five.
  • The mechanism behind it, measured rather than assumed — a flat $0–50 per claim on one side, a deductible and annual cap on the other.
  • Where a blended figure would mislead. Commercial members span 6.7% to 74.4% by care type, government members 0.6% to 8.1%; the blend describes neither.

Finding 4Spending concentrates in places, not in people

What this shows. 86 of our 3,918 facilities carry half of everything we spend — 2.2% of sites. Reaching the same half through members takes 134,198 people, 10.7% of the membership. Measured fairly, as a share of each group, sites are about five times more concentrated than members.

Why it matters for Calder. It settles where our attention is worth spending. 86 sites is a list a team can genuinely work through one at a time. 134,198 members is not a list at all — it is a small city, and no case-management programme reaches it. This is the one thing this analysis establishes that looking at conditions or costs alone never could, because neither counts people.

What we examined

  • Concentration measured four ways — by member, by site, by condition, by type of care — to check the answer was not an artefact of one grouping.
  • Whether comparing 86 against 134,198 is fair. It is not, on its own: there are 321 times more members than sites, so the fair comparison is 2.2% against 10.7%.
  • How our member concentration compares with real claims data. Ours is flatter — our top 1% carry 11.8% where real books run 20–25% — so if anything this understates how few members matter.
  • Whether the site concentration is actionable, which is Finding 5.

Finding 5Our most expensive hospitals are not charging more

What this shows. Cost per member runs from $4,401 to $144,422 across facilities — a 33-fold spread. But reprice every procedure in the data to a single common rate and that spread barely moves, from 15.8× to 15.2×. A median of just 7.8% of a site’s cost per visit reflects what it charges; the rest is what it treats. The same holds for how much gets done: for the typical condition, facilities perform near-identical amounts of work.

Why it matters for Calder. The obvious reading of a 33-fold spread — that some hospitals overcharge — is wrong, and acting on it would waste a planning cycle. The sites that look most expensive per member are running dialysis three times a week at ordinary prices. The ones that look most expensive per visit are hospices, where a multi-week stay is billed as a single visit. Even pregnancy, the one condition where facilities genuinely differ, splits into delivery units and antenatal clinics doing different halves of the same care. There is no pricing problem here to find.

What we examined

  • Whether the spread is price or case mix, by repricing every procedure to its all-facility average and remeasuring. It is case mix.
  • Whether facilities differ in how much they do for the same condition. For the typical condition, no — and the one big exception is pregnancy.
  • What separates cheap from expensive pregnancy sites. At the cheapest, 95.5% of women give birth on site; at the most expensive, 6.8%. Delivery units against antenatal clinics.
  • The two groups that stand out on the chart, both identified by facility name rather than by anything in the data itself.
