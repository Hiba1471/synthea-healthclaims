What changed on the Q3 charts

The report used to recommend negotiating hospital prices. Testing it showed prices barely vary between hospitals — a median 7.8% of a site’s cost per visit is what it charges. The replacement idea, reviewing how much work hospitals do, tested away too. Both charts were still arguing for those. Here is what moved.

The hospital chart

Opening note
before

Led with the hospice explanation. Nothing said the chart had no lever.

after

There is no cost lever on this chart. Reprice every procedure in the dataset to its all-hospital average and the spread barely moves — 15.8× down to 15.2×. A median of just 7.8% of a site's cost per visit reflects what it charges rather than what it does. Hospitals here differ in the work they perform, never in how efficiently they perform it, so the horizontal axis is a measure of case mix wearing a price label (q3_price_vs_casemix_2020_2024.csv).

Second note
before

Did not exist.

after

The same holds for how much they do. Across 119 conditions the typical spread in procedures per claim between hospitals is 0.027 — near-identical. The one big exception, normal pregnancy, splits into sites that deliver babies ($4,130 a claim) and sites that run antenatal clinics ($17,315); nine months of appointments against one birth, not waste (q3_utilisation_or_composition_2020_2024.csv).

The concentration chart

Opening note
before

Did not exist. The chart showed a list of 86 sites and implied you would act on it.

after

This list is a scope, not a lever. The 86 sites are worth knowing because they are few. What to do with them is a separate question, and four candidate answers have each been tested and ruled out: case management (there is no small patient group — that is this chart), price negotiation (a median 7.8% of a site's cost per visit is its prices), chronic-care management at the busiest sites (those visits are dialysis), and utilisation review (hospitals do near-identical amounts of work). Acting on the list needs a way to compare like with like — severity adjustment, real negotiated rates, or outcomes — which this dataset does not carry.

Nothing about the data changed. Every dot sits where it always did, and both headlines are untouched — “The dearest hospitals charge ordinary prices” was already saying the right thing.

What changed is what the charts claim you can do. Before, they framed two groups of hospitals as two problems to fix. Now they say those hospitals are doing different work, not doing it badly — and that there is no cost lever on the chart at all.

The concentration chart kept its finding and lost its implication. 86 sites still carry half the money. But it now says that list is a scope rather than a lever, and names the four programmes that were each tested and ruled out, so nobody reinvents one.
