The cheaper the care, the more of the bill patients carry

Synthea healthcare claims, 2020–2024. Each circle is one of the 15 types of care, placed by what the care costs per person and by how much of that bill the patient pays. Circle size is the number of people affected.

Thirteen of fifteen types follow the pattern — two run against it

The dashed line is a least-squares guide: every tenfold increase in cost per person comes with about 9 points less patient share. It accounts for 44% of the variation (rank correlation −0.60), so the pattern is real but loose — more than half of what moves patient share is something other than price.

The two purple circles are the counter-examples. Infections costs $24,713 a person and members still carry 28.0%: expensive care with a routine-care share. Brain & nervous system costs $8,939 and members carry only 8.5%: cheap care with a catastrophic-care share. Neither category is price-corrected, so both read the same here as they do as billed. Everything else lines up: maternity at $19,284 a person sits near the floor at 17.0%, while blood disorders and diabetes at under $1,900 sit at the top. Allergy is the circle to watch. As billed it sat with maternity near the floor at 10.0% on $175,111 a person; it carries the largest documented pricing gap in this dataset, roughly 89 times, and corrected it lands at 15.0% on $2,432, which moves it most of the way across this chart.

Limitations

  • A guide line, not a model — 15 points, and price explains under half the spread.
  • Association only; nothing here shows that price causes the share.
  • Log x-axis — equal spacing means ten times the cost, not ten more.
  • Other is a 38-condition catch-all, so its position averages unlike things.
  • Shares are blends across insurance type; commercial patients pay far more than government ones.
